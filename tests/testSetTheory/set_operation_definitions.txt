(*
The declared set operations -- Union, Intersection, PowerSet, CartesianProduct
and Pair -- mean nothing by themselves; five rules give them their meaning, each
by citing one defining sentence: (Definition of union), (Definition of
intersection), (Definition of power set), (Definition of Cartesian product) and
(Definition of ordered pair). Each rule takes the sentence for any terms, with or
without leading foralls (the form `Set property` reads, the instance for one
element, or the sentence closed over its variables), and takes nothing else: a
true sentence about the operation is derived from the definition, not cited as it.

Proofs 1-5 show the forms each rule accepts. Proofs 6-13 prove facts about the
operations with them: union commutes, a union contains each of its sets, an
intersection is inside each of its sets, the empty set belongs to every power set,
equal ordered pairs have equal components, and the Cartesian product has exactly
the pairs of members. Proof 8 cites proof 7 by its title, as any theorem is. The
invalid proofs are sentences close to a definition that are not it, among them the
two in which a quantifier of the sentence would capture a variable of a term.
*)

# 1: Union, in the forms the rule accepts
## Proof that
### the union of A and B has the members of A and of B, however the definition is written
1. Let A, B be any set, and x be any object. (Declaration)
2. forall u, (In(u, Union(A, B)) iff (In(u, A) or In(u, B))). (Definition of union)
3. In(x, Union(A, B)) iff (In(x, A) or In(x, B)). (Definition of union)
4. forall S, forall T, forall u, (In(u, Union(S, T)) iff (In(u, S) or In(u, T))). (Definition of union)
5. (In(x, A) or In(x, B)) iff In(x, Union(A, B)). (Definition of union)
6. x is in A union B iff (x is in A or x is in B). (Definition of union)
7. In(x, Union(Union(A, B), EmptySet)) iff (In(x, Union(A, B)) or In(x, EmptySet)). (Definition of union)

# 2: Intersection, in the forms the rule accepts
## Proof that
### the intersection of A and B has the members common to A and B, however the definition is written
1. Let A, B be any set, and x be any object. (Declaration)
2. forall u, (In(u, Intersection(A, B)) iff (In(u, A) and In(u, B))). (Definition of intersection)
3. In(x, Intersection(A, B)) iff (In(x, A) and In(x, B)). (Definition of intersection)
4. forall S, forall T, forall u, (In(u, Intersection(S, T)) iff (In(u, S) and In(u, T))). (Definition of intersection)
5. (In(x, A) and In(x, B)) iff In(x, Intersection(A, B)). (Definition of intersection)
6. x is in A intersection B iff (x is in A and x is in B). (Definition of intersection)

# 3: The power set, in the forms the rule accepts
## Proof that
### the power set of A has the subsets of A as its members, however the definition is written
1. Let A be any set, and x be any object. (Declaration)
2. forall u, (In(u, PowerSet(A)) iff u subseteq A). (Definition of power set)
3. In(x, PowerSet(A)) iff x subseteq A. (Definition of power set)
4. forall u, (In(u, PowerSet(A)) iff (forall z, (In(z, u) -> In(z, A)))). (Definition of power set)
5. forall S, forall u, (In(u, PowerSet(S)) iff u subseteq S). (Definition of power set)
6. (forall z, (In(z, x) -> In(z, A))) iff In(x, PowerSet(A)). (Definition of power set)
7. x is in the power set of A iff x is a subset of A. (Definition of power set)

# 4: The Cartesian product, in the forms the rule accepts
## Proof that
### the Cartesian product of A and B has the pairs of a member of A and a member of B, however the definition is written
1. Let A, B be any set, and p be any object. (Declaration)
2. forall q, (In(q, CartesianProduct(A, B)) iff exists a in A, exists b in B, q = Pair(a, b)). (Definition of Cartesian product)
3. In(p, CartesianProduct(A, B)) iff (exists a in A, exists b in B, p = Pair(a, b)). (Definition of Cartesian product)
4. In(p, CartesianProduct(A, B)) iff (exists s, (In(s, A) and exists t, (In(t, B) and p = Pair(s, t)))). (Definition of Cartesian product)
5. forall S, forall T, forall q, (In(q, CartesianProduct(S, T)) iff exists a in S, exists b in T, q = Pair(a, b)). (Definition of Cartesian product)
6. (exists a in A, exists b in B, p = Pair(a, b)) iff In(p, CartesianProduct(A, B)). (Definition of Cartesian product)

# 5: Ordered pairs, in the forms the rule accepts
## Proof that
### ordered pairs are equal exactly when their components are, however the definition is written
1. Let a, b, c, d be any object. (Declaration)
2. Pair(a, b) = Pair(c, d) iff (a = c and b = d). (Definition of ordered pair)
3. forall x, forall y, (Pair(a, b) = Pair(x, y) iff (a = x and b = y)). (Definition of ordered pair)
4. forall w, forall x, forall y, forall z, (Pair(w, x) = Pair(y, z) iff (w = y and x = z)). (Definition of ordered pair)
5. (a = c and b = d) iff Pair(a, b) = Pair(c, d). (Definition of ordered pair)
6. Pair(a, Pair(b, c)) = Pair(d, Pair(a, b)) iff (a = d and Pair(b, c) = Pair(a, b)). (Ordered pair equality)

# 6: Union is commutative
## Proof that
### then A union B = B union A.
1. Let A, B be any set. (Declaration)
2. forall u, (In(u, Union(A, B)) iff (In(u, A) or In(u, B))). (Definition of union)
3. forall u, (In(u, Union(B, A)) iff (In(u, B) or In(u, A))). (Definition of union)
4. A union B is a subset of B union A. (Subset proof below)
 4.1. Let x in A union B. (Assumption for subset proof)
 4.2. In(x, A) or In(x, B). (Set property from 2, 4.1)
 4.3. In(x, B) or In(x, A). (Proof by Cases from 4.2, subproofs below)
 begin subproof
  4.3.1. In(x, A). (Case)
  4.3.2. In(x, B) or In(x, A). (Disjunction Introduction from 4.3.1)
 end subproof
 begin subproof
  4.3.1. In(x, B). (Case)
  4.3.2. In(x, B) or In(x, A). (Disjunction Introduction from 4.3.1)
 end subproof
 4.4. In(x, B union A). (Set property from 3, 4.3)
5. B union A is a subset of A union B. (Subset proof below)
 5.1. Let x in B union A. (Assumption for subset proof)
 5.2. In(x, B) or In(x, A). (Set property from 3, 5.1)
 5.3. In(x, A) or In(x, B). (Proof by Cases from 5.2, subproofs below)
 begin subproof
  5.3.1. In(x, B). (Case)
  5.3.2. In(x, A) or In(x, B). (Disjunction Introduction from 5.3.1)
 end subproof
 begin subproof
  5.3.1. In(x, A). (Case)
  5.3.2. In(x, A) or In(x, B). (Disjunction Introduction from 5.3.1)
 end subproof
 5.4. In(x, A union B). (Set property from 2, 5.3)
6. A union B = B union A. (Set Equality from 4, 5)

# 7: A union contains its first set
## Proof that
### If A and B are sets then A is a subset of A union B.
1. Let A, B be any set. (Declaration)
2. forall u, (In(u, Union(A, B)) iff (In(u, A) or In(u, B))). (Definition of union)
3. A is a subset of A union B. (Subset proof below)
 3.1. Let x in A. (Assumption for subset proof)
 3.2. In(x, A) or In(x, B). (Disjunction Introduction from 3.1)
 3.3. In(x, A union B). (Set property from 2, 3.2)

# 8: A union contains its first set, for other sets
## Proof that
### If C and D are sets then C is a subset of C union D.
1. Let C, D be any set. (Declaration)
2. C is a subset of C union D. (A union contains its first set)

# 9: An intersection is inside its first set
## Proof that
### If A and B are sets then A intersection B is a subset of A.
1. Let A, B be any set. (Declaration)
2. forall u, (In(u, Intersection(A, B)) iff (In(u, A) and In(u, B))). (Definition of intersection)
3. A intersection B is a subset of A. (Subset proof below)
 3.1. Let x in A intersection B. (Assumption for subset proof)
 3.2. In(x, A) and In(x, B). (Set property from 2, 3.1)
 3.3. In(x, A). (Conjunction Elimination from 3.2)

# 10: The empty set belongs to every power set
## Proof that
### If A is a set then the empty set is a member of the power set of A.
1. Let A be any set. (Declaration)
2. forall u, (In(u, PowerSet(A)) iff u subseteq A). (Definition of power set)
3. The empty set is a subset of A. (Subset proof below)
 3.1. Let x in the empty set. (Assumption for subset proof)
 3.2. x is not in the empty set. (Set property)
 3.3. x is in A. (Explosion from 3.1, 3.2)
4. In(EmptySet, PowerSet(A)). (Set property from 2, 3)

# 11: Ordered pairs with equal pairs have equal first components
## Proof that
### If Pair(a, b) = Pair(c, d) then a = c.
1. Let a, b, c, d be any object. (Declaration)
2. Pair(a, b) = Pair(c, d). (Premise)
3. Pair(a, b) = Pair(c, d) iff (a = c and b = d). (Definition of ordered pair)
4. Pair(a, b) = Pair(c, d) -> (a = c and b = d). (Biconditional Elimination from 3)
5. a = c and b = d. (Modus Ponens from 4, 2)
6. a = c. (Conjunction Elimination from 5)

# 12: A pair of members belongs to the Cartesian product
## Proof that
### If a is in A and b is in B then Pair(a, b) is in the Cartesian product of A and B.
1. Let A, B be any set, and a, b be any object. (Declaration)
2. In(a, A). (Premise)
3. In(b, B). (Premise)
4. forall p, (In(p, CartesianProduct(A, B)) iff exists x in A, exists y in B, p = Pair(x, y)). (Definition of Cartesian product)
5. Pair(a, b) = Pair(a, b). (Reflexivity)
6. In(b, B) and Pair(a, b) = Pair(a, b). (Conjunction Introduction from 3, 5)
7. exists y, (In(y, B) and Pair(a, b) = Pair(a, y)). (Existential Introduction from 6)
8. In(a, A) and exists y, (In(y, B) and Pair(a, b) = Pair(a, y)). (Conjunction Introduction from 2, 7)
9. exists x, (In(x, A) and exists y, (In(y, B) and Pair(a, b) = Pair(x, y))). (Existential Introduction from 8)
10. In(Pair(a, b), CartesianProduct(A, B)). (Set property from 4, 9)

# 13: The components of a pair in the Cartesian product are members
## Proof that
### If Pair(a, b) is in the Cartesian product of A and B then a is in A and b is in B.
1. Let A, B be any set, and a, b be any object. (Declaration)
2. In(Pair(a, b), CartesianProduct(A, B)). (Premise)
3. forall p, (In(p, CartesianProduct(A, B)) iff exists x in A, exists y in B, p = Pair(x, y)). (Definition of Cartesian product)
4. exists x, (In(x, A) and exists y, (In(y, B) and Pair(a, b) = Pair(x, y))). (Set property from 3, 2)
5. Let x be an object. (Declare)
6. In(a, A) and In(b, B). (Existential Elimination from 4, subproof below)
begin subproof
 6.1. In(x, A) and exists y, (In(y, B) and Pair(a, b) = Pair(x, y)). (Assumption for Existential Elimination)
 6.2. In(x, A). (Conjunction Elimination from 6.1)
 6.3. exists y, (In(y, B) and Pair(a, b) = Pair(x, y)). (Conjunction Elimination from 6.1)
 6.4. Let y be an object. (Declare)
 6.5. In(a, A) and In(b, B). (Existential Elimination from 6.3, subproof below)
 begin subproof
  6.5.1. In(y, B) and Pair(a, b) = Pair(x, y). (Assumption for Existential Elimination)
  6.5.2. In(y, B). (Conjunction Elimination from 6.5.1)
  6.5.3. Pair(a, b) = Pair(x, y). (Conjunction Elimination from 6.5.1)
  6.5.4. Pair(a, b) = Pair(x, y) iff (a = x and b = y). (Definition of ordered pair)
  6.5.5. Pair(a, b) = Pair(x, y) -> (a = x and b = y). (Biconditional Elimination from 6.5.4)
  6.5.6. a = x and b = y. (Modus Ponens from 6.5.5, 6.5.3)
  6.5.7. a = x. (Conjunction Elimination from 6.5.6)
  6.5.8. b = y. (Conjunction Elimination from 6.5.6)
  6.5.9. In(a, A). (Substitution from 6.5.7, 6.2)
  6.5.10. In(b, B). (Substitution from 6.5.8, 6.5.2)
  6.5.11. In(a, A) and In(b, B). (Conjunction Introduction from 6.5.9, 6.5.10)
 end subproof
end subproof
